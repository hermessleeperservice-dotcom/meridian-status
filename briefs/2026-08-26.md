# Brief 2026-08-26

**Design's core problem is "legibility," not budget**

Peter Merholz argues that businesses can only value what they can see, and most of design's highest-impact work fails that test. 
Design struggles to be legible within businesses because so many of its practices are indeterminate, and in most companies the only legible aspect of design becomes "screens given to engineering."
 His fix is to make design's practices, outputs and outcomes visible on the terms the business already understands, not to wait for the business to change its terms.
So what: before restructuring the design function, map which parts of its work are currently "board feet" to the business and which are invisible, because that gap is what will keep starving headcount and mandate regardless of org chart.

[Peter Merholz](https://www.petermerholz.com/newsletter/bridging-the-legibility-gap/)

**Engineering leaders are quitting the jobs everyone else wants**

Gergely Orosz reports a wave of CTOs, VPEs and Heads of Engineering walking away from senior roles, driven less by burnout than by founders' unrealistic AI expectations. 
More CTOs, VPEs, and Heads of Engineering are walking away from their high-status, in-demand positions, for reasons mostly related to AI and to "founder mode."
 One departing CTO describes the real strain as managing "AI psychosis" in executive peers rather than the technology itself.
So what: as leadership expects visible AI transformation fast, the same dynamic risks landing on design leadership too, so it's worth pre-empting what "AI-native design org" is being asked to mean before someone above you decides for you.

[The Pragmatic Engineer](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/the-great-engineering-leader-career-break)

**Incentives, not technology, decide who wins the agentic era**

Ben Thompson's latest essay uses the cowboy-hat cliché of old Westerns to argue that agentic cybersecurity dynamics favour attackers structurally, which will "limit incumbents and fuel startups in the long run." 
Incentives favor offense when it comes to agentic cybersecurity, and it's the same dynamic that will limit incumbents and fuel startups in the long run.

So what: the same incentive logic applies inside a large retailer adopting agentic tools in personalisation and loyalty, where the fast-moving challenger integration will often beat the cautious incumbent one unless governance is deliberately designed to remove that asymmetry.

[Stratechery](https://stratechery.com/2026/autonomy-and-innovation/)

**Brussels starts actually enforcing the AI Act**

From 2 August 2026 the EU AI Office gained real teeth. 
Europe's fight to regulate AI models moved from paper to practice on 2 August 2026, and new transparency rules took effect requiring certain AI systems to tell users when they're interacting with AI and when content has been generated or altered by it.
 
Chatbots have to identify themselves as automated systems, deepfakes need a label, and machine-made content must carry machine-readable marks, with fines up to €15 million or 3% of worldwide turnover for non-compliance.

So what: any customer-facing agent, meal planner or chat-based personalisation surface now needs an explicit "you are talking to AI" disclosure baked in as a design requirement, not an afterthought, for any EU-facing surface.

[Help Net Security](https://www.helpnetsecurity.com/2026/08/04/eu-ai-act-enforcement-ai-models/)

**AI winning the A/B test isn't the same as AI being the right call**

Byrne Hobart's roundup makes a sharp point about authenticity in an AI-saturated market. 
If AI wins A/B tests, it can still be a strategically sound decision not to use it.
 He frames this alongside a broader observation that 
it's probably load-bearing that companies can turn money into changes in consumer perception.

So what: when AI-generated personalisation or copy outperforms in short-run metrics, that's not sufficient grounds to deploy it broadly if it erodes the brand's perceived authenticity over a longer horizon than the test window captures.

[The Diff](https://www.thediff.co/archive/who-hedges-where/)

**Airline loyalty AI amplifies whatever the programme already optimises for**

A loyalty-industry analysis of comments from Flying Blue, Sixt and Miles & More argues personalisation is being treated as an end in itself rather than an output of genuine customer understanding. 
Personalisation should be seen as an output, not the objective, because airlines already sit on vast quantities of loyalty data and the constraint is not volume but signal, which AI does not inherently improve, it only amplifies whatever structure already exists.
 
As one industry figure puts it, "Loyalty is optimised for extraction, not for learning."

So what: before layering AI onto Clubcard-style personalisation, audit whether the underlying programme is structured to learn about customers or simply to extract more from them, because AI will make either tendency more efficient, not correct it.

[RevMan on Substack](https://revman.substack.com/p/how-ai-exposes-weaknesses-in-airline)

**A satirist's brush with AI-personalised scam mail reframes the trust problem**

Ted Gioia recounts receiving what appeared to be a personalised pitch from a Hollywood name, engineered with intimate knowledge of his own back catalogue. 
He describes getting an email purportedly from an actress wanting to adapt his book, one that clearly knew his book and described it in painful detail, written without a single grammatical error or typo.
 The piece is less about celebrity impersonation and more about how AI has industrialised hyper-personalised deception at the exact moment brands are being told to personalise more.
So what: as personalisation becomes the default expectation of good CX, the same technology is lowering the cost of personalised fraud, which strengthens the case for visible provenance and authentication cues in any AI-touched customer communication.

[The Honest Broker](https://www.honest-broker.com/p/reese-witherspoon-and-me)

**A roadmap essay for AI governance treats "no plan" as the real risk**

Scott Alexander's write-up of the AI Futures Project's "Plan A" reframes AI policy debate away from prediction and toward institutional design, arguing most fights over speed miss the deeper structural risk. 
Plan A isn't another prediction, it's a wish list, a positive vision, a road map for navigating the future, describing the best course of action the authors can come up with and what would happen if it were taken.
 
The piece's blunt conclusion is that nobody has a plan for if this AI thing turns out to be real.

So what: the same absence of an operating plan shows up inside large organisations adopting AI piecemeal, so the essay is a useful prompt to ask whether your own design and research function has a "Plan A" for AI rather than a series of pilots.
